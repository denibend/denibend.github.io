Study 01 · Luxury research series

What drives European luxury stocks?

Market, currency and China exposure, creative-director and CEO changes, and the post-2023 divergence of eight European luxury groups, 2014–2026.

Denisa Bendaj · Draft of 2026-10-23 · Data to 2026-10-23
Draft for review — not published. Findings are preliminary and may change after review.

Executive summary

  • The market dominates, but most weekly movement is company-specific. The European market, EUR/USD and Chinese equities together explain 17–46% of weekly returns. Luxury has also become more market-sensitive: the median market beta rose from 0.85 (2014–19) to 1.34 (2023–26).
  • A stronger euro did not hurt luxury stocks week to week; the sign is the opposite. The translation effect, if it exists, is not visible in weekly prices.
  • China matters, but less than it did. Exposure to Chinese equities is significant for 5 of 8 stocks over the full sample (7 of 8 with the MSCI China ETF). It peaked in 2020–22 and has largely faded since.
  • Since 2023 the slowdown has been both a sector shock and a brand story. Every stock shares a drag of roughly −28 to −50 log points versus factor-implied returns. On top of that, brand-specific gaps range from Kering (−100) to Richemont (+43).
  • Creative-director changes move stocks only where the brand is the profit engine. Demna's appointment at Gucci cost Kering −13.0% on the day; Tisci's arrival at Burberry added 5.2%. LVMH designer changes barely registered. At Kering, CEO changes moved the stock more than most creative changes, but the sample is small.

Research question and hypotheses

How much of European luxury stock performance is explained by common market forces (the equity market, the euro and China), and how much by company-specific events such as creative and management changes?

HypothesisVerdict
H1 · China exposure matters beyond the marketPartly supported (5 of 8; faded after 2022)
H2 · A stronger euro hurts returnsNot supported (opposite sign at weekly frequency)
H3 · The post-2023 divergence is mainly company-specificPartly supported (sector-wide drag plus large brand gaps)
H4 · Creative-director changes move the stockSupported only where the brand dominates group profit
H5 · Top-tier and aspirational brands divergeSupported descriptively; the gap opened in 2020–22

Background

European luxury groups are among the largest listed companies in Europe. After a decade of strong returns, the sector slowed from 2023 and its stocks diverged widely. Market commentary usually explains these moves with the Chinese consumer, currency swings, or creative turnover. In 2024–26 Gucci, Bottega Veneta, Balenciaga, Dior and Saint Laurent all changed creative leadership. This study tests how much of the stock behaviour those explanations actually account for.

Sample: January 2014 to 2026-10-23 (669 weeks); data retrieved 2026-10-23. Checks found no missing weeks or stale prices. All ten daily moves larger than 15% were checked against news. Each coincides with a results day, a corporate announcement, a short-seller report or a market-wide shock. Yahoo's STOXX 600 series lacks 64 trading days since 2014; the event study handles this by aligning prices before computing returns. Raw provider data is not redistributed.

Methodology

  • Factor model: weekly (Friday-to-Friday) log returns in euros, regressed on the STOXX 600, the change in EUR/USD and the Hang Seng, with Newey–West standard errors. Weekly sampling limits the bias from markets that close at different times.
  • Stability: sub-periods (2014–19, 2020–22, 2023–26) and 104-week rolling windows.
  • Post-2023 divergence: sensitivities estimated on 2014–22 only; afterwards, the gap between actual and factor-implied returns. The pre-2023 excess return (alpha) is deliberately not extrapolated, because assuming luxury's 2014–22 outperformance continued would make any slowdown look like a collapse. A leave-one-out peer factor (the other seven stocks) splits the gap into sector-wide and brand-specific parts.
  • Event study: daily market model estimated over the trading year before each announcement (days −250 to −21). Abnormal returns on the announcement day and over days −1…+1.
  • Robustness: MSCI China ETF as an alternative China proxy; local-currency returns for non-euro listings.

Since 2023 hard luxury (Richemont) has kept rising (46%) and the top tier is modestly up (10%), while the aspirational (−30%) and diversified (−50%) groups have fallen. The gap between top-tier and aspirational brands opened earlier than the slowdown, in 2020–22.

Four announcements stand out: Demna to Gucci (−13.0%, t −5.9), Tisci to Burberry (+5.2%), Bizzarri leaving Gucci (+6.8%) and Luca de Meo named Kering CEO (+10.9%). None of the four LVMH designer changes was significant.

Interpretation

Common factors set the tide, not the outcome. The market explains most of the shared movement, and macro factors are second-order. Most of what separates winners from losers is company-specific.

The euro result runs against intuition. A plausible reading is that a weaker dollar coincides with global risk appetite and stronger purchasing power for dollar-linked buyers. The currency translation hit arrives through reported earnings over quarters, which weekly prices do not isolate. Testing this properly requires earnings-revision data.

China has become a fundamentals story. The fading link to Chinese equities after 2022 is consistent with Chinese demand now reaching luxury stocks through results and guidance rather than through day-to-day market sentiment. Another possibility is that the Hang Seng has become a weaker proxy for the wealth of Chinese luxury buyers.

The slowdown hit everyone, and positioning decided how hard. Hard luxury and top-tier names held up better than aspirational fashion brands. This is consistent with the view that aspirational buyers retreated first, but it is not proof of it. LVMH's large brand-specific gap is notable for a diversified group and may reflect divisions outside fashion; segment data would be needed to tell.

Investors price creative change only where it is material. When one brand drives group profit, a designer appointment is treated as strategy: the Demna reaction reflects scepticism at announcement, not a verdict on the collections. The larger reactions to Kering's CEO changes fit a company in turnaround, where investors may reward leadership change itself.

What would weaken these conclusions: results driven by the choice of window or benchmark. Robustness checks so far (alternative China proxy, local currencies, sub-periods) do not change the picture. A market index that excludes luxury has not yet been tested.

Limitations

  • Associations, not causal effects. Weekly co-movement does not measure fundamental exposure.
  • The STOXX 600 contains several of the stocks studied, which inflates their measured market sensitivity.
  • Brand-specific gaps are relative to peers (one peer's collapse lifts the others), and the 2014–22 benchmark assumes stable sensitivities.
  • Event study: 20 events, possible leaks before announcements, and same-day news (COVID on 24 Feb 2020; the Hang Seng crash on 8 Oct 2024). With many tests, only the largest reactions are robust.
  • Yahoo Finance is not an audited source; every daily move larger than 15% was checked against news.

Next steps

  • Re-estimate with a European market index that excludes luxury.
  • Event study on results days, to test whether China now reaches the stocks through earnings.
  • Segment and regional revenue from company filings, to explain the brand-specific gaps (LVMH in particular).
