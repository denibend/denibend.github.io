The luxury wallet

Wealth vs income, goods vs experiences, and the regional mix of luxury demand in the United States, Europe and China.

Executive summary

  • Luxury-type spending follows stock-market wealth. In the US, a typical quarterly move in household equity wealth (88% of it held by the top 10%) goes with {{ h1.jw_equity|num("%.1f") }}% more jewellery and watch spending over the following year, against {{ h1.goods_equity_us|num("%.1f") }}% for goods in general. EU clothing-type goods show a similar equity effect ({{ h1.sd_equity_eu|num("%.1f") }}% against {{ h1.cl_equity_us|num("%.1f") }}% in the US).
  • But categories mix wallets. US jewellery is also the most income-sensitive category (elasticity ≈ {{ h1.jw_income_el|num("%.1f") }}), and EU clothing-type goods depend on income as much as on wealth. Price tiers within a category serve different buyers.
  • Consumer confidence now separates top-tier from aspirational luxury stocks. Since 2022, a typical monthly confidence move shifts the gap between the two tiers by ~{{ h2.typical_move|num("%.1f") }}% (t {{ h2.late_t|num("%.1f") }}). Before 2020 there was no such link.
  • No general shift from goods to experiences. US experiences regained their 2019 share of spending but sit {{ (-h3.exp_share_dev)|num("%.0f") }}% below their pre-COVID trend. Air travel ({{ h3.air_dev|num("%+.0f") }}%) and jewellery ({{ h3.jw_dev|num("%+.0f") }}%) are both above trend: a complement pattern, not substitution. France and Spain show a shift to services; Germany and Italy do not.
  • China's luxury demand travels, and spends less per trip. Hong Kong jewellery and watch sales move with mainland visitor numbers and the renminbi. Sales per mainland visitor fell from HK${{ h3.pv_peak|thousands }} ({{ h3.pv_peak_year }}) to HK${{ h3.pv_last|thousands }} ({{ h3.pv_last_year }}), a record low.
  • Regional mix does not explain winners and losers. Asia exposure in 2022 does not predict the post-2023 brand-specific gap (ρ = {{ h4.rho|num("%+.2f") }}, permutation p = {{ h4.p|num("%.2f") }}, 8 groups).

H1 · Luxury-type goods respond more to wealth than to income; within categories, high-priced items follow top-of-distribution wealth and low-priced ones follow income Partly supported: strong equity-wealth link in the US and the EU; categories blend both wallets
H2 · Top-tier luxury stocks track wealth, aspirational ones track income and confidence Supported since 2022 for confidence; income result not robust
H3a · Income-driven wallets shift from goods to experiences Not visible in US aggregates; visible in France and Spain; income-group test pending
H3b · Wealth-driven wallets add experiences and keep buying (complement) Consistent with US trends, Hong Kong data and company commentary; not detectable in EU category data
H4 · More Asia exposure in 2022 → larger post-2023 brand-specific gap Rejected

Background

Luxury sales slowed after 2023, and the usual explanations point in different directions: a squeezed aspirational middle class, a post-COVID preference for travel and experiences, and weaker Chinese demand. Study 01 found that common market factors explain less than half of luxury stock movements. This study looks behind the stocks at the consumer: whose wallet funds luxury spending, what it competes with, and where it comes from.

Methodology

  • Wealth vs income: quarterly growth of real category spending regressed on income growth and on equity and housing wealth growth, each with four lags (US 1990–2026, n = {{ h1.n_us }}; EU panel of seven countries, n = {{ h1.n_eu }}, with country fixed effects), excluding 2020–21. Wealth groups (top 1% vs next 9%) move too closely together to be separated, so the study splits wealth by asset type: equities (88% held by the top 10%) vs housing (the middle class's main asset).
  • Typical-quarter effect: elasticity × one standard deviation of the driver's quarterly growth, so drivers that move by different amounts can be compared.
  • Tiers: monthly top-tier minus aspirational return on market, China, EUR/USD, EU confidence and US income, by sub-period.
  • Goods vs experiences: real volumes and spending shares against log-linear 2010–19 trends; EU annual panel of category spending on foreign tourist nights.
  • China via Hong Kong: year-on-year growth of jewellery sales on mainland visitors, renminbi buying power, the Shanghai Composite and gold (2005–2026, excluding the border closure; n = {{ h3.n_hk }}), plus a model fitted on 2005–19 to measure the post-reopening gap.
  • Regional mix: Asia (incl. Japan) share of revenue, which every company's reporting supports, against Study 01's brand-specific gap; Spearman correlation with a permutation test (n = 8).

Interpretation

The wealthy wallet sets the tone for luxury goods, but no category belongs to one wallet. Equity wealth is the strongest single driver of US jewellery, watch and leather-goods spending, consistent with demand concentrated among households whose wealth is in financial markets. The large income response of jewellery fits the price-tier view: the same category holds pieces bought from salary and pieces bought from portfolios.

Experiences complement luxury rather than replace it, at least at the top. In the US, travel and jewellery both run above trend while hotels and package tours do not, so the money is not visibly leaving luxury goods for experiences. Hong Kong, where luxury demand is tied to visitor flows, and company commentary (Kering: Western Europe weakened on "low numbers of tourists, particularly from Asia"; Cucinelli: Europe supported by "high-end tourism flows") point the same way. The substitution story fits France and Spain, and remains to be tested by income group.

The aspirational consumer became a market factor after 2022. Before the pandemic, confidence did not separate the tiers; since 2022 it does, and strongly. Top-tier names fall slightly when confidence rises, which suggests investors treat them as a safe haven within luxury.

Where a company sells matters less than what it sells. Richemont (48% of revenue in Asia) outperformed its peers by the widest margin, and Hermès, the most Asia-exposed group (57%), stayed close to its peers, far ahead of Kering (38%) and LVMH (37%). Combined with the tier and confidence results, the evidence points to positioning and client mix, not geography, as the source of the post-2023 divergence.

Limitations

  • Associations, not causal effects. Equity wealth also captures confidence and expectations.
  • Official categories are broader than luxury, and EU jewellery & watches sit inside durables (dominated by cars).
  • EU category data record purchases where they happen, so tourist spending is included.
  • The US jewellery price index barely reflects the 2024–26 gold surge, so real jewellery volumes may be overstated.
  • Hong Kong sales are nominal and include gold jewellery; visitor data are by nationality since October 2024.
  • The regional dataset combines different company region definitions and fiscal years; see the data file for per-row notes.
  • Short samples: the post-2022 confidence regime is 56 months; the regional cross-section is 8 companies.
